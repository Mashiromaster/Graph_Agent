\begin{abstract}
Comparisons between multi-agent LLM topologies are difficult to reproduce.
Wall-clock concurrency, per-arm differences in budget accounting, and divergent
partial-failure handling all perturb the outcome, so a reported topology effect
is confounded with runtime nondeterminism and with unequal effective budgets.
We present an agent-graph runtime built so that \emph{the runtime contributes
zero entropy}: given a recorded sequence of model responses, the final run
snapshot is reproducible bit-exactly regardless of concurrency, scheduling, or
completion order. Three properties make this possible --- order-invariant
super-step merging, fail-closed all-or-nothing ingestion of agent proposals, and
a budget ledger that enforces parity by construction rather than by post-hoc
filtering. Treating the runtime as a measurement instrument, we then ask what
agent-graph mechanisms actually buy at matched token budget, ablating weighted
scheduling, budget allocation, cross-generation skip context, and message
passing as independent toggles with everything else held bit-identical. Across
118 live cells, decomposition improves quality but costs $2.3$--$3.1\times$ the
tokens, so the single-agent baseline wins under almost any non-zero cost
weighting; of the communication mechanisms only declarative data flow carries
the effect, with relevance-routed delivery indistinguishable from its absence;
and the whole effect is conditional on the model electing to decompose: on the
tier whose orchestrators declined to delegate in 94\% of eligible cells, no
topology arm beat the baseline.
All experiments ship with replay transcripts, so every number can be re-derived
offline at zero API cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A claim of the form ``this agent topology outperforms that one'' carries an
implicit premise: that the measured difference came from the topology. Three
things make that premise hard to secure. Wall-clock concurrency determines the
order in which agent results are merged, and if the merge is order-sensitive the
final state depends on scheduling. Budget accounting differs between arms, so a
configuration that spawns more agents may also have spent more, and the
comparison silently becomes quality-per-unequal-budget. Partial failures are
handled differently across runtimes --- dropped, retried, or propagated --- so
an arm that fails more often can look better or worse for reasons unrelated to
its topology.

Our own experiments supplied an unplanned illustration. Eight separate
infrastructure defects produced results that read as ``multi-agent arms perform
worse'': a context role the endpoint rejected, child specifications missing a
required field, children inheriting a shorter timeout than their parent, an
output-length cap below what the task needed, a per-minute token quota, retry
backoff accumulating past a node deadline, a client library's non-configurable
header timeout, and a scorer that read only the root agent's text. Each hurt the
arms with more agents and more turns first, because those arms reach any new
ceiling first. The failures therefore arrived sorted by arm complexity --- which
is exactly the shape a real topology effect would have. One of them, the
root-only scorer, would have inverted our headline finding: re-scoring the same
transcripts under the corrected rule moved the single-agent arm by $0.000$ and
every spawning arm by $+0.155$ to $+0.396$.

We take the position that the instrument must be fixed before the measurement
means anything, and that fixing it is a contribution in its own right. We
present an agent-graph runtime with three properties.

\paragraph{The runtime contributes zero entropy.}
This needs stating precisely, because the obvious stronger version is false. At
temperature $>0$ two live runs of the same configuration do not agree, and we do
not claim they do. What we claim and verify is that the \emph{runtime} is not a
source of variation: fixing the model's responses fixes the final snapshot
digest exactly, under any concurrency limit and any completion order
(Theorem~\ref{thm:order}). All remaining entropy is attributable to the model,
which is what makes a topology comparison meaningful. Across 118 live cells
every group of repeated runs agreed on its digest, and recorded transcripts
replay to their live digests bit-exactly.

\paragraph{Agents propose; only the controller mutates.}
A model's graph operations are proposals, re-validated at a single ingestion
boundary and materialised by the controller. Ingestion is fail-closed and
all-or-nothing: an invalid result adopts none of its proposals. An agent cannot
drive the graph into a state the policy forbids, so topology stays inside the
configured envelope regardless of what the model emits.

\paragraph{Budget parity holds by construction.}
All arms share one token ceiling, enforced by a reservation ledger on every
termination path (Theorem~\ref{thm:budget}) rather than by discarding overspent
runs afterwards. The single-agent baseline is not a cheaper control; it is the
same budget spent without decomposing.

Using the runtime as an instrument, we measure what agent-graph mechanisms buy
at matched budget. Decomposition helps and costs far more than it returns: every
spawning arm beats the single-agent baseline, but for $2.3$--$3.1\times$ the
tokens, and the cost-weight crossover sits at $\lambda \approx 0.01$, so the
baseline wins under almost any non-zero cost weighting. Among the communication
mechanisms, declarative data flow carries the effect and relevance routing does
not: disabling \texttt{depends\_on} propagation collapses A5's advantage from
$+0.118$ to a non-significant $+0.047$, while disabling relevance-routed
delivery barely moves it. And the effect is conditional on the model choosing to
decompose --- on the tier whose orchestrators declined to delegate in 94\% of
eligible cells, no arm beat the baseline.

That last result is why we report spawn rate beside every quality number. Where
the orchestrator does not spawn, a topology arm is operationally identical to the
baseline, and ``no topology effect'' becomes indistinguishable from ``this task
did not need topology''. Task decomposability is a precondition for the question
being falsifiable, not a detail of experimental setup.

All experiments ship with replay transcripts, so every number here can be
re-derived offline at zero API cost. We used that capability ourselves to
quantify the scorer bias above.

Two lines of work bracket this one. \emph{Recursive runtimes} ---
LangGraph~\citep{langgraph}, AutoGen's GraphFlow~\citep{wu2023autogen},
MetaGPT~\citep{hong2024metagpt}, CrewAI~\citep{crewai},
CAMEL~\citep{li2023camel}, Swarm~\citep{openaiswarm} --- let agents spawn agents
at runtime, but the resulting structure is a tree or a predefined workflow with
one undifferentiated edge kind, and nothing optimises it.
\emph{Graph-optimisation work} --- GPTSwarm~\citep{zhuge2024gptswarm},
DyLAN~\citep{liu2024dylan}, AgentPrune~\citep{zhang2024agentprune},
G-Designer~\citep{zhang2024gdesigner}, MacNet~\citep{qian2024macnet} --- treats
the topology as a learnable object, but fixes it before execution, so the graph
cannot respond to what agents discover mid-run.
AutoAgents~\citep{chen2023autoagents} generates roles at runtime without
optimising the connections between them.

Our mechanisms are not novel against that second group; weighted edges, pruning
and context routing all appear there. What is absent from both groups is the
measurement apparatus. None of these systems claims that its final state is
independent of completion order, and none enforces a shared budget ceiling
across the configurations it compares --- the two properties that decide whether
a reported topology difference is attributable to topology. Our empirical
findings are also mostly consonant with theirs rather than competing:
AgentPrune's result that pruning edges preserves quality while cutting cost sits
naturally beside our finding that the arms do not separate from each other while
costing $2.3$--$3.1\times$ the baseline.

The typed-edge column marks a smaller contribution that turned out to matter.
Separating scheduling edges from information edges, and mandatory payloads from
discretionary ones, is what makes §\ref{sec:results}'s ablation able to
attribute the effect to declarative data flow rather than to ``communication''
in general.

\section{Runtime model and determinism}
\label{sec:runtime}

\subsection{Attributed graph over time}

A run is a sequence of graphs $G_t = (V_t, E_t, X_t, W_t)$ indexed by super-step
$t$, where $V_t$ are agent nodes, $E_t \subseteq V_t \times V_t$ carry a type
label, $X_t$ assigns each node its input context, and $W_t$ assigns scalars to
nodes and edges. The sequence is monotone in $V$ and $E$: nodes and edges are
only added, never removed, so the graph doubles as an execution trace and the
audit chain can record state transitions rather than deletions.

\paragraph{Four edge types, not one.}
Edges differ along two independent axes, and collapsing them loses the
distinction the experiments need:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Type & Triggers execution & Carries payload & Payload gated by \\
\midrule
\texttt{spawn}        & yes & parent's instructions   & --- \\
\texttt{depends\_on}  & yes & source's actual output  & nothing (mandatory) \\
\texttt{message}      & no  & proposed content        & relevance, trust \\
\texttt{skip\_context}& no  & ancestor's output       & relevance, trust, gate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\texttt{spawn} and \texttt{depends\_on} gate \emph{scheduling} --- a node is
ready only when its dependencies have succeeded --- while \texttt{message} and
\texttt{skip\_context} gate \emph{information} without affecting readiness. A
single fused edge type would force every information flow to also be a
scheduling constraint, which rules out the sibling-to-sibling and
ancestor-to-descendant deliveries that §\ref{sec:results} ablates.

The second axis is whether the payload is discretionary. \texttt{depends\_on}
is a declaration by the graph author that the target must see the source's
output; a relevance score has no standing to veto it. The other two carry
optional evidence, which is exactly what routing should filter. Our results turn
on this split: the mandatory channel carries the measured effect and the
discretionary one does not. We found the distinction the hard way --- an earlier
design gated both through the same relevance score, and fan-in silently
delivered nothing whenever a stemming mismatch drove that score to zero.

\paragraph{Cycles.}
Agents may revisit each other, so the natural communication graph is cyclic,
but execution must be a DAG for the merge order of Theorem~\ref{thm:order} to be
well-defined. We unroll: a node identity is
$(\mathit{agent\_id}, \mathit{round}, \mathit{invocation\_id})$, so a cycle
$A \to B \to A$ materialises as $A_1 \to B_1 \to A_2$ with distinct nodes. The
logical cycle is preserved in the trace while the executed graph stays acyclic,
and node identifiers --- derived by hashing their parts --- stay unique without a
counter that concurrency could interleave.

A consequence worth stating because it shapes §\ref{sec:mechanisms}: under this
model a node executes exactly once and never re-enters. Anything that would
require re-entry, such as a node revising its output after reading a child's,
has to be expressed as a new node rather than a second visit.

\subsection{Proposals and the trust boundary}

Agents cannot mutate the graph. A model's \texttt{graph\_\_*} tool calls produce
only \emph{proposals} in the returned result; the controller re-validates every
proposal at ingestion and materialises nodes and edges itself. Ingestion is
fail-closed and all-or-nothing: an invalid result adopts \emph{none} of its
proposals and commits its entire token reservation.

\subsection{Super-step execution}

Execution proceeds in BSP super-steps~\citep{valiant1990bsp} with three phases:
freeze the ready frontier and claim reservations under a lock; execute claimed
runners concurrently with \emph{no} lock held; re-acquire the lock and merge
outcomes \emph{ordered by claim sequence rather than completion order}.

\begin{theorem}[Order-invariant merge]
\label{thm:order}
Fix a root specification, a controller policy, and the multiset of agent results
produced in a run. The final snapshot is independent of the order in which
runners complete and of the concurrency limit.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The merge phase is a fold over the claimed executions, and we show both the
sequence folded over and the step function are independent of completion order.

\emph{The sequence is fixed before execution begins.} Each claim receives
\texttt{execution.sequence} from a counter incremented inside the critical
section of the freeze phase, so sequence numbers are assigned by frontier
position, not by when a runner returns. The merge sorts outcomes by that field
before applying any of them. Completion order therefore influences only when an
outcome becomes available, never where it sits in the fold.

\emph{Each step is a deterministic function of (state, result).} Records are
immutable: mutators replace whole records through factories rather than
assigning fields, so no step can observe a partially-updated predecessor.
Identifiers are derived by hashing their semantic parts
(\texttt{deriveControllerId}), not from counters that concurrent execution could
interleave. Proposals within a result are processed in the fixed order spawn,
message, skip. There are no reads of wall-clock time or randomness on this path.

\emph{The frontier is a function of state.} Ready nodes are ordered by
$(-\mathit{priority}, \mathit{nodeId})$, a total order because node identifiers
are unique, so the next super-step's claim sequence is itself determined by the
merged state rather than by scheduling.

By induction over super-steps, the state entering each merge is fixed, the
sequence folded over is fixed, and each step is deterministic; hence the final
snapshot is fixed. The concurrency limit changes only how many runners are
in flight, which the argument never uses.
\end{proof}

The tie-break deserves emphasis because it is the part that can silently stop
being load-bearing. With distinct priorities the ordering is determined by
priority alone and the identifier comparison never executes, so a test built on
distinct-priority nodes will pass even if the tie-break is replaced by a random
comparator. We verified the property by mutation testing with equal priorities,
where removing the deterministic tie-break makes the digests diverge.

\begin{theorem}[Budget parity]
\label{thm:budget}
On every termination path --- success, structured failure, invalid result,
runner exception, cancellation, and dependency block --- committed tokens never
exceed the configured limit, and reserved tokens return to zero at convergence.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The ledger holds two counters, $C$ (committed) and $R$ (reserved), and
maintains the invariant $C + R \leq L$ for the configured limit $L$.

Tokens enter the ledger only at node creation, which reserves a worst-case
allocation: the reservation is rejected unless $C + R + r \leq L$, so the
invariant is preserved on entry. Claiming a node for execution moves its
reservation out of the per-node map but leaves $R$ unchanged --- the tokens stay
reserved while the runner is in flight --- so claiming cannot violate the
invariant either.

Every reservation then leaves the ledger by exactly one of three transitions,
and each decreases $R$ by the full reserved amount $r$:
\begin{description}
\item[commit-success] $R \mathrel{-}= r$; $C \mathrel{+}= \min(\max(a, 0), r)$
      for actual usage $a$. The clamp is what makes this safe: a model
      over-reporting usage cannot commit more than was reserved, and the unused
      remainder is released implicitly.
\item[commit-full] $R \mathrel{-}= r$; $C \mathrel{+}= r$. Used for structured
      failure, invalid results, and runner exceptions --- work was performed,
      so the whole reservation is charged.
\item[release] $R \mathrel{-}= r$; $C$ unchanged. Used for cancellation and
      dependency blocking, where the node never ran.
\end{description}
In each case the change to $C + R$ is at most zero, so the invariant survives.
The six termination paths of the theorem map onto these three: success to
commit-success; structured failure, invalid result and runner exception to
commit-full; cancellation and dependency block to release.

For the second claim, convergence requires every node to be in a terminal state
or to have never been reserved. Each reserved node reaches exactly one of the
three transitions above, each of which subtracts its full $r$ from $R$, and
reservations are per-node and never shared. Hence $R = 0$ at convergence.
\end{proof}

Two consequences matter for the experiments. Because an invalid result commits
its entire reservation rather than releasing it, a model cannot gain budget by
emitting proposals that fail validation. And because the limit is enforced on
entry rather than checked afterwards, arms are comparable without discarding
overspent runs --- a filter that would otherwise correlate with arm complexity,
since the arms that spawn more are the ones that would overspend.

\subsection{Audit chain and resumability}

Every run appends to a hash chain: each event stores the SHA-256 of its own
canonicalised fields together with its predecessor's hash, starting from a fixed
genesis value. Verification recomputes the chain, so any edit to a recorded event
invalidates every hash after it.

The chain stores \emph{digests only}, never plaintext. A node's event records the
hash of its input and output rather than the text, and the controller's events
record state transitions rather than content. This is what lets the audit trail
be published alongside a paper without publishing the prompts and completions,
and it is also why delivered message content is kept outside the snapshot: a
message edge stores $\mathrm{sha256}(\mathit{content})$, while the content
itself reaches the target through a buffer that the snapshot projection does not
include. Delivery therefore cannot perturb the state digest, which we rely on
when claiming that adding delivery left the checkpoint tests byte-identical.

\paragraph{Two checkpoint schemas.}
Version 1 is pure snapshot replay: it carries the event chain and a state
digest, and replay re-verifies the chain, a topology digest, and the snapshot's
semantic invariants. It is a static function of the chain --- it never touches a
model, a runner, or a tool --- so verifying a checkpoint costs nothing and
cannot have side effects.

Version 2 adds a resumable slice: per-node specifications, inputs, tool names
and reservations, plus the archived results of completed executions. Its first
six keys are byte-identical to v1, so a v2 checkpoint verifies as a v1
checkpoint. On resume, completed nodes are trusted from the archive --- records
rebuilt, results restored, no runner constructed, no reservation taken, no
re-billing --- while a node interrupted mid-execution is reset to ready and
re-runs. Resume is fail-closed: four pre-validations run before anything is
constructed, including that reservations of incomplete nodes sum to the recorded
reserved total, so a truncated or edited checkpoint is rejected rather than
half-loaded.

Resumability is not a convenience feature here. The grid in
§\ref{sec:design} ran for days against a rate-limited endpoint and was
interrupted repeatedly; without idempotent re-entry keyed on completed cells,
each interruption would have cost the whole run.

\section{Mechanisms under study}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

\subsection{Four disjoint scalars}

\begin{description}
\item[\texttt{priority}] orders the ready frontier and nothing else.
\item[\texttt{budget\_share}] sizes a child's token allocation.
\item[\texttt{context\_gate}] bounds how much of an edge's payload may enter the
      target's context.
\item[\texttt{trust}] gates and ranks delivery.
\end{description}

Keeping these disjoint is deliberate: a single fused ``weight'' conflates
scheduling, economics, information flow, and credibility, after which no
ablation can attribute an effect to any one of them.

\paragraph{Why trust learning must be cross-run and role-keyed.}
This is a structural consequence worth stating as a result rather than a
limitation. In a runtime where a node executes exactly once and never re-enters,
within-run node-level trust learning is impossible by construction: for node
$n$'s trust to influence any decision it would have to be updated after $n$
produces its result and then read again, but $n$ never runs again, and its
children froze their identities at spawn time. The only feasible learning is
therefore across runs and aggregated by role. We use an interpretable
exponential moving average, $\mathrm{trust}^{(t+1)} = (1-\alpha)\,
\mathrm{trust}^{(t)} + \alpha\, r^{(t)}$, and explicitly decline end-to-end
reinforcement learning at this stage; credit assignment is by leave-one-out
role ablation.

\subsection{Typed context delivery}

Context reaches a node at one point: the moment its frontier position is frozen,
immediately before it runs. Three channels feed that point.

\texttt{depends\_on} propagation supplies the source's \emph{actual output},
taken from the archive of settled results. This is what turns a dependency edge
from a pure scheduling barrier into a data-flow edge, and it is the mechanism
that gives fan-in: a parent spawns $N$ workers plus a reducer with
\texttt{depends\_on} edges from each, and the reducer sees all $N$ outputs. Under
the single-execution model of §\ref{sec:runtime} there is no other way for a
parent's work to reach a sibling, because by the time children run the parent is
already terminal.

Message delivery supplies proposed content to a target that has not yet run. A
message to a node already running or finished is rejected rather than recorded,
since it provably cannot influence any output; making that rejection explicit
keeps ineffective communication visible in the decision log instead of leaving
an inert edge that looks like successful delivery.

Skip context supplies an ancestor's output to a descendant, gated by the
proposal's \texttt{context\_gate}. Because the requesting node has by definition
already run, a parent may request skip context on behalf of a child it spawned
in the same result; otherwise every skip edge would be structurally dead on
arrival.

\paragraph{Routing and the token knapsack.}
Discretionary deliveries are scored
$s_{u \rightarrow v} = \mathit{priority}_u \cdot \mathrm{rel}(u, v) \cdot
\mathit{trust}_{\mathrm{role}(u)} \cdot \mathit{gate}$, ranked, truncated to
Top-$K$, then packed greedily against a token budget. Ties break on edge
identifier so the selection is a total order, which Theorem~\ref{thm:order}
requires. Mandatory deliveries bypass ranking and zero-score filtering but still
charge the token budget --- otherwise a graph could evade budget parity by
declaring dependencies.

The minimum-trust gate is applied \emph{before} scoring and overrides the
mandatory flag. An injected low-trust agent therefore cannot force its output
into a descendant's context by declaring a dependency edge, which is the
property the robustness arm was built to test.

Relevance is a deterministic lexical IDF-weighted cosine over the run's own
text. Embeddings are excluded deliberately: they would add a runtime dependency
and, served remotely, place a source of nondeterminism at the centre of the
zero-entropy claim. The cost is real and we report it as such --- lexical
relevance is a lower bound on what routing can achieve, so the null result in
§\ref{sec:results} bounds this router rather than context routing in general.

Routing scores a candidate delivery as
$s_{u \rightarrow v} = \mathrm{priority}_u \cdot \mathrm{rel}(u,v) \cdot
\mathrm{trust}_{\mathrm{role}(u)}$, selects Top-$K$, then fills a token budget
greedily. Relevance is a deterministic lexical IDF-weighted cosine. Embeddings
are excluded on purpose: they would add a runtime dependency and, if served
remotely, a source of nondeterminism at the centre of the zero-entropy claim.
We therefore report lexical relevance as a \emph{lower bound} on what routing
can achieve, which makes a positive result conservative and obliges us to hedge
a null one.

\subsection{Why black-box skip connections are semantic, not residual}

Skip connections in a neural network add a tensor: $h_{l+1} = F(h_l) + h_l$,
with addition well-defined because both operands inhabit the same hidden space.
Behind a black-box API none of that holds. There are no hidden states to read,
no shared representation across agents, and no addition operator on text. Work
that calls agent-graph edges ``residual connections'' is using an analogy, and
the analogy breaks precisely where it would do work: there is no identity path
along which a signal passes unchanged.

What is achievable is a \emph{semantic} skip, and we name it \texttt{skip\_context}
rather than residual to avoid the implication. An ancestor's output is filtered
for relevance, allocated a share of the target's context budget, and injected
as \emph{evidence} rather than as instruction. That last step is a safety
property, not a formatting choice: delivered content from another agent is
untrusted input, so it is wrapped with its provenance --- source node, edge type,
trust score --- and the node's own instructions remain the only system-level
text. A delivery that arrived as a system instruction would let any agent in the
graph rewrite any other agent's objective.

Tensor-level residuals would require self-hosting homogeneous models, a shared
tokenizer and hidden dimension, and end-to-end differentiability through the
graph. That is a different research programme --- model architecture rather than
orchestration --- and we do not attempt it.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Arms and toggles}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Experimental arms. All arms share one token ceiling, enforced by the
controller's ledger rather than by discarding overspent runs.}
\label{tab:arms}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.2cm}}
\toprule
Arm & Label & Increment \\
\midrule
A1 & single agent & spawn tool withheld; \texttt{maxNodes}~$=1$ \\
A2 & equal-weight tree & $+$ spawn, uniform \texttt{budget\_share}, fixed \texttt{priority} \\
A3 & weighted tree & $+$ model-chosen \texttt{priority} and \texttt{budget\_share} \\
A4 & fixed skip graph & $+$ cross-generation skip context \\
A5 & dynamic graph & $+$ messages and role-keyed trust \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two orthogonal toggles, \textsc{fan-in} and \textsc{delivery}, ablate the
mechanisms behind the communication claim. The delivery toggle is implemented by
closing the context-injection token budget rather than by branching, so control
and treatment traverse identical code paths and differ only in available budget.

\subsection{Tasks, scoring, and budget parity}
\label{sec:graph-scoring}

Scorers are deterministic pure functions. This is load-bearing rather than
fastidious: variance is a reported metric, so a stochastic scorer would mix
scorer variance into the quantity we attribute to the model. That requirement is
also the honest reason only two task families survive --- unit-testable code
generation~\citep{chen2021humaneval,liu2023evalplus} and mathematics with
deterministic answers~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}.

\subsection{Objective and the weight sweep}

We report $R = \mathrm{Quality} - \lambda\,\mathrm{Cost} - \mu\,\mathrm{Latency}
- \nu\,\mathrm{Failure}$, but treat the Pareto front over quality and cost as
the primary figure. The reason is empirical, not stylistic: in pilot runs the
ranking of arms under $R$ flips as $\lambda$ crosses a threshold, so any single
choice of $\lambda$ would be an unjustifiable degree of freedom. We therefore
sweep $\lambda/\mu/\nu$ and report whether conclusions survive the sweep.

a tier whose zero scores come from truncation would have produced the finding
``stronger models do worse at multi-agent tasks'' --- an artifact. Cross-model
claims in this paper therefore span two tiers.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Determinism validation}

Across all 118 cells, every group of repeated runs sharing a condition, task,
seed and model produced an identical snapshot digest: 118 of 118 groups agree,
zero inconsistencies. Recorded transcripts replayed offline reproduce their live
digests bit-exactly under varying concurrency limits and artificially shuffled
completion orders.

We report this as a stop-the-line check rather than a summary statistic. A rate
below 100\% would mean an undiscovered source of nondeterminism and would
invalidate Theorem~\ref{thm:order}; there would be no point reporting the
mechanism results that follow, because the runtime would be contributing
variance of its own.

per thousand tokens --- A1 wins and keeps winning through $\lambda = 0.2$. The
Pareto front over quality and cost retains four non-dominated conditions (A1,
A3, A5 without delivery, A5), so this is a genuine trade-off rather than one arm
dominating. We therefore treat the front as the primary result and $R$ as a
summary, because no defensible argument picks a single $\lambda$.

So the mechanism that matters is declarative data flow --- a child seeing a

evidence. This is measurable only because the two were separated: an earlier
design gated both through the same relevance score, which made fan-in fail
whenever a stemming mismatch drove the score to zero. Had they stayed fused
behind a single ``communication'' switch, the ablation would have reported one
blended number and attributed the effect to the wrong thing.

The null result on routing should be read against its implementation: relevance
is lexical IDF cosine, deliberately not semantic (\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}). It is
a lower bound on what routing could achieve, so this is evidence against
\emph{this} router, not against context routing in general.

\subsection{The effect is conditional on the model choosing to decompose}

This is the most consequential result in the dataset, and it is a conditional
one.

On the small tier \emph{no} arm beats A1: the paired differences are $+0.000$,

every arm does. The dividing variable is not the model's capability but its
\emph{behaviour}: the small tier declined to delegate in 94\% of eligible cells, judging
--- in its own words --- that decomposition would add overhead without reducing
risk. An arm whose orchestrator does not spawn is operationally identical to A1,
so the small tier largely measures A1 against itself.

Grouping by behaviour rather than by tier makes this direct. Among spawning-arm
cells, those that actually spawned average $0.834$ quality ($n = 49$) against
$0.635$ for those that did not ($n = 51$) --- a gap of $0.199$, larger than any
between-arm difference we measured.

Two consequences follow. First, the pooled $+0.118$ in Table~\ref{tab:main} is
an average over a population that is 42\% treated, and it understates the effect
where decomposition occurs while overstating it where it does not. Second, task
decomposability and the model's willingness to exploit it are preconditions for
topology effects being measurable at all; without them, ``no topology effect''
is indistinguishable from ``this task did not need topology'' and is not
falsifiable. We report spawn rate alongside every quality number for this
reason.

We also note what this costs the cross-model claim. With two tiers and the
effect present in one, we cannot separate ``mid-specific'' from
``spawning-specific'', since the tiers differ in both scale and spawn rate. The
honest statement is that the effect tracks spawn rate, and that we lack the tiers
to decide whether anything else tracks scale.

\subsection{Not measured}

Two planned results are absent. Weight mechanisms (\texttt{priority} versus
\texttt{budget\_share} versus role trust) are not separable in this dataset: no
pairwise difference among A2--A5 is significant, and A2 --- which forces uniform
weights --- is statistically indistinguishable from A3, which lets the model
choose them. We can report that disjoint weight semantics are implementable and
auditable, not that any particular weighting helps.

The injected-faulty-agent arm was built (\S\ref{sec:mechanisms}) but not run,
because the grid consumed the available quota. It needs no new mechanism, only
compute.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Scope of the determinism claim.}
It is conditional on the model. We claim zero runtime entropy, not bit-identical
live runs, and the distinction is load-bearing: a reader who takes the stronger
reading will find it false at the first temperature-$0.7$ run.

\paragraph{One task family, one effect size.}
Measurements come from a single family of decomposable technical briefs, scored
by keyword coverage. Keyword coverage rewards breadth of mechanism naming and is
insensitive to whether the prose around each mechanism is correct, so it
measures something narrower than quality. A code-generation family with
unit-test scoring was built, including a sandboxed executor, but not run for
lack of quota; the mathematics family was dropped after measurement showed the
model declines to decompose arithmetic, which makes every arm identical to the
baseline. Our effect sizes are therefore specific to tasks whose output volume
exceeds one context window.

\paragraph{Two tiers cannot separate scale from behaviour.}
The effect appears on the mid tier and not the small tier, but those tiers differ
in both capability and spawn rate (84\% against 5\%). We can say the effect
tracks spawn rate; we cannot say whether anything independently tracks scale. A
third tier was attempted and excluded (\S\ref{sec:design}), so this gap is known
rather than overlooked.

\paragraph{Mechanisms we could not separate.}
No pairwise difference among A2--A5 is significant, so the weight results are
null: A2, which forces uniform weights, is statistically indistinguishable from
A3, which lets the model choose them. We report that disjoint weight semantics
are implementable and auditable, not that any weighting helps. Relevance routing
is lexical IDF cosine by design, so its null result bounds \emph{this} router
rather than context routing generally. Trust learning is cross-run and
role-keyed, which follows from the single-shot node model and cannot be loosened
without changing it. No learned topology optimiser is included. The
faulty-agent robustness arm is built but unrun.

\paragraph{The code-generation scorer is process isolation, not a sandbox.}
It executes model-generated code in a separate subprocess with network and
subprocess modules blocked, wall-clock and memory limits, a whitelisted
environment, and a disposable working directory. The child still runs as the
invoking user. We state this plainly rather than describing the setup as
sandboxed, because a reader reusing the artifact on untrusted payloads needs a
container or VM instead.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

We set out to make agent-topology comparisons mean what they appear to mean. The
runtime contributes zero entropy given fixed model responses, enforces budget
parity by construction, and confines agents to proposals that a single ingestion
boundary validates. Across 118 live cells those properties held: every group of
repeated runs agreed on its snapshot digest, and recorded transcripts replay to
their live digests bit-exactly.

What the instrument then measured is less flattering to graph machinery than the
literature's framing suggests. Decomposition helps --- every spawning arm beats
the single-agent baseline --- but for $2.3$--$3.1\times$ the tokens, with the
cost-weight crossover at $\lambda \approx 0.01$, and the arms do not separate
from each other. Of the communication mechanisms, only declarative data flow
carries the effect; relevance-routed delivery is indistinguishable from its
absence. Most importantly, the effect is conditional on the model electing to
decompose at all: where it declined, no topology arm beat the baseline, and
cells that actually spawned outscored those that did not by more than any
difference between arms.

We think the methodological result is the more durable one. Eight infrastructure
defects in this project produced data that read as ``multi-agent arms perform
worse'', each because the arms with more agents and more turns reach any new
ceiling first --- so the failures arrived sorted by arm complexity, the same
shape a real topology effect has. One of them would have inverted our headline
finding. Published without the instrument, any of them would have been a
plausible, citable, wrong conclusion. That is the case for treating
reproducibility infrastructure as a result rather than as plumbing, and for
shipping transcripts so that a reader who doubts a number can recompute it
instead of trusting us.

\section{Rejection codes at the ingestion boundary}
\label{app:codes}

Twenty-four codes are enforced when a result is ingested. They are grouped here
by what they protect, because the grouping is the design: an agent cannot reach
a forbidden state through any single path, and a result that trips any code
adopts \emph{none} of its proposals.

\paragraph{Result integrity (6).}
\texttt{MALFORMED\_RESULT}, \texttt{INVALID\_NODE\_STATUS},
\texttt{INVALID\_USAGE}, \texttt{RESULT\_BINDING\_MISMATCH},
\texttt{INVALID\_EVENT\_CHAIN}, \texttt{INVALID\_EVENT\_SCHEMA}.
\texttt{RESULT\_BINDING\_MISMATCH} is the load-bearing one: a result carries a
hash binding its node identifier, status, output, usage and all three proposal
lists, so a result cannot be edited in transit or replayed against a different
node. \texttt{INVALID\_USAGE} rejects reported usage exceeding the reservation,
which is what stops a model from over-reporting to inflate its own budget.

\paragraph{Structural limits (6).}
\texttt{NODE\_LIMIT}, \texttt{EDGE\_LIMIT}, \texttt{CHILD\_LIMIT},
\texttt{DEPTH\_LIMIT}, \texttt{PROPOSAL\_LIMIT}, \texttt{GENERATION\_MISMATCH}.
These bound the graph independently of what the model requests, which is why
arm A1 is single-agent by construction rather than by the model's cooperation:
its policy sets \texttt{maxNodes} to one, so a spawn proposal is rejected even
if the tool were somehow reachable.

\paragraph{Topology validity (5).}
\texttt{SELF\_EDGE}, \texttt{TARGET\_NOT\_FOUND}, \texttt{SOURCE\_MISMATCH},
\texttt{DUPLICATE\_PROPOSAL}, \texttt{TARGET\_ALREADY\_EXECUTED}.
The last is a consequence of single execution (§\ref{sec:runtime}): a message to
a node already running or finished provably cannot affect any output, so it is
rejected rather than recorded. Without it an inert edge would appear in the
graph and read as successful delivery.

\paragraph{Policy and permission (4).}
\texttt{POLICY\_DENIED}, \texttt{PERMISSION\_ESCALATION},
\texttt{INVALID\_BUDGET\_SHARE}, \texttt{TOKEN\_BUDGET\_EXHAUSTED}.
\texttt{PERMISSION\_ESCALATION} rejects a child requesting tools its parent
lacks, so authority is monotonically non-increasing down the graph.

\paragraph{Execution faults (3).}
\texttt{FACTORY\_ERROR}, \texttt{INVALID\_EVENT\_STATE},
\texttt{MALFORMED\_PROPOSAL}.

\section{Event chain and checkpoint schemas}
\label{app:schemas}

\paragraph{Event chain.}
Ten node-level event types are recorded: \texttt{NODE\_STARTED},
\texttt{NODE\_COMPLETED}, \texttt{NODE\_FAILED}, \texttt{BUDGET\_EXCEEDED},
\texttt{MODEL\_STARTED}, \texttt{MODEL\_COMPLETED}, \texttt{TOOL\_STARTED},
\texttt{TOOL\_COMPLETED}, \texttt{TOOL\_REJECTED},
\texttt{PROPOSAL\_CREATED}. Each event stores
$\mathrm{sha256}$ of its canonicalised fields together with its predecessor's
hash, from a genesis value of 64 zero characters. Input and output appear as
hashes, never as text.

A second chain at controller level records run lifecycle transitions
(\texttt{run\_created}, \texttt{snapshot}, \texttt{dependency\_added},
\texttt{execution\_settled}). Both chains route every hashed field through one
shared helper, so recording and verification cannot drift apart --- a defect
that would otherwise be invisible until a verification failure in production.

\paragraph{Checkpoint v1.}
Six keys: \texttt{chainHead}, \texttt{eventCount}, \texttt{events},
\texttt{runId}, \texttt{schemaVersion}, \texttt{stateDigest}. Replay is a static
function of the chain: it re-verifies the hash chain, a topology digest, and the
snapshot's semantic invariants, and never constructs a model, runner or tool.

We state a limit the design does not overcome. The chain detects accidental
corruption and in-chain tampering, but it does not prove provenance: anyone able
to recompute the chain can forge a consistent one. Cross-host deployment would
need HMAC or KMS signing with a persisted head anchor.

\paragraph{Checkpoint v2.}
The same six keys byte-for-byte, with \texttt{schemaVersion} set to 2, plus a
seventh \texttt{resumable} key carrying per-node specifications, inputs, tool
names and reservations, the archived results of settled executions, and the
enqueue sequence. A v2 document therefore verifies as a v1 document.

Agent specifications are stored opaquely because the orchestrator package
deliberately does not depend on the node package; resume takes a rehydration
callback supplied by the one component that depends on both. Parsing is
defensive: a 5\,MB cap, full-depth duplicate-key rejection, exact top-level
key-set equality, and NaN/Infinity rejection.

\section{Mechanisms considered and declined}
\label{app:declined}

Three mechanisms appear in the surrounding literature and in our own design
notes but are deliberately absent. We record them because declining them is a
claim about scope that a reader should be able to check.

\paragraph{Learned topology optimiser.}
A component that proposes graph edits from observed reward, as in
GPTSwarm~\citep{zhuge2024gptswarm} and G-Designer~\citep{zhang2024gdesigner}.
Declined because the measurement problem had to be settled first. An optimiser
trained against a biased signal optimises the bias --- had we built one before
finding the root-only scoring defect (§\ref{sec:intro}), it would have learned
to suppress spawning, and the resulting graphs would have looked like evidence
for a conclusion that was an artifact.

\paragraph{Hidden-state residual connections.}
Declined as infeasible rather than unattractive, for the reasons in
§\ref{sec:mechanisms}: black-box APIs expose no hidden states, agents share no
representation, and text admits no addition operator. Implementing it would
require self-hosted homogeneous models with a shared tokenizer and hidden
dimension, plus differentiability through the graph --- a model-architecture
programme rather than an orchestration one.

\paragraph{End-to-end reinforcement learning for credit assignment.}
Declined on the grounds that a good final answer does not establish that each
participating agent contributed; an agent may restate its predecessor and still
share the reward. We use leave-one-out role ablation and an interpretable EMA on
role trust instead. This is also the honest reading of our own weight results:
with no pairwise difference among A2--A5 reaching significance, we have no
signal that would supervise an optimiser.

We note the common thread. Each of these mechanisms needs a trustworthy reward
or quality signal, and this paper's main empirical finding is how fragile that
signal was --- eight infrastructure defects, one of which inverted the headline
result. Building optimisers on top of a signal that required this much work to
stabilise seemed premature.
